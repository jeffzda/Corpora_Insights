# Coupled-Objective Trade-off (p18): When Two Goals Share One Knob

## 1. Framing

A coupled-objective trade-off is the failure pattern that emerges when two performance objectives share a structural coupling such that improving one necessarily degrades the other. It is not an engineering oversight to be designed away; it is a binding constraint inherited from physics, market architecture, contract structure, or system topology. In the ARENA portfolio, this archetype shows up when a single asset is asked to deliver two services that compete for the same finite resource — capacity headroom, electrical current, photons, thermal flux, reactor residence time, regulator attention, customer trust — and where the optimisation frontier between them is convex enough that no single operating point is dominant.

The archetype matters for a renewables-investment portfolio because it sits underneath many of the apparent disappointments of demonstration projects: a wind farm's FCAS trial earning less than the energy it curtails to deliver the service<sup>1,2</sup>, a grid-forming battery whose inertial response weakens precisely as it earns more from energy arbitrage<sup>3,4</sup>, a tandem solar cell whose top sub-cell improvements never translate into module gains because the bottom sub-cell limits current<sup>5</sup>. In each case the project did what was technically asked of it, but the headline metric fell short because the engineering or market structure forced a compromise the business case had not internalised.

The evidence base spans 393 records across 153 projects in 17 ARENA categories, with project years from 2010 to 2025 and publish dates from 2012 to 2026. The 24 constituent clusters cover physical-engineering trade-offs (FCAS headroom vs energy output, photon absorption vs voltage in tandems, kinetic vs thermodynamic optima in chemistry), system-architectural ones (reactive vs real power in inverters, ancillary reservation vs market dispatch), and human/regulatory ones (granularity vs simplicity in tariff design, accuracy vs accessibility in compensation). The breadth itself is informative: where so many distinct technical communities independently rediscover the same shape of problem, the underlying mechanism is structural rather than circumstantial.

## 2. The Mechanism at the Parent Level

The unifying causal pathway can be stated compactly. A system possesses a single binding resource — call it R — that is required for two distinct objectives, A and B. The total demand for R from A and B exceeds the available R at the operating points where both A and B are most valuable. The system is therefore on the Pareto frontier between A and B, and any choice of operating point sacrifices some quantity of one for the other. Crucially, the trade-off is *not* fixable by better engineering of A or B in isolation, because the constraint is on R, not on the conversion of R into A or B.

The shape of R varies by sub-pattern. In FCAS-from-renewables, R is the headroom between current output and maximum available power<sup>1,2,6</sup>. In grid-forming inverters, R is rated kVA and overcurrent margin<sup>4,7</sup>. In tandem solar cells, R is the photon flux through each spectral band, allocated by sub-cell bandgap<sup>5,8</sup>. In ammonia synthesis, R is reactor temperature, which sets both kinetic rate and equilibrium yield in opposing directions<sup>9</sup>. In dynamic network tariff design, R is the regulator's attention and consumer comprehension budget<sup>10,11</sup>.

Where the mechanism depends on time-variant context, the dependencies are real but not always destabilising. Market-rule-based trade-offs (FCAS vs energy co-optimisation) shifted with the introduction of five-minute settlement and changing FCAS market design from 2017 onward. Contract-based trade-offs (RERT availability vs FCAS bidding) reflected the specific NER 3.20.3(j) interpretation operative in 2017–2018<sup>12</sup>. Physical trade-offs in semiconductors and reactor chemistry are time-invariant. The synthesis below names where the trade-off is binding under any plausible future state versus where it is artefact of a particular regulatory or market epoch.

## 3. Sub-Patterns: How the Coupled-Objective Trade-off Manifests

The 24 clusters group naturally into six sub-patterns of expression, each illuminating a different setting where two valued objectives must share one resource.

### 3.1 Headroom-as-resource: ancillary services from energy-bound assets

The largest and most operationally consequential sub-pattern in the corpus involves variable renewable generators and battery storage attempting to provide raise-direction frequency services while also maximising energy revenue. The constituent clusters [c534] and [c948] each describe the same coupling from different angles: raise FCAS requires headroom between current output and capacity, and that headroom is the very thing that energy dispatch consumes.

The Hornsdale Wind Farm 2 FCAS trial provides the canonical evidence base. Average curtailment to provide raise regulation FCAS was 18.7 MW against an average dispatch of only 12.4 MW<sup>1</sup>; the trial earned $24,000 in FCAS revenue against $83,000 of curtailed energy plus LGC value<sup>13</sup>; and 11% of dispatch intervals saw the wind farm stranded outside its FCAS trapezium because its forecast output drifted across the enablement boundary<sup>2</sup>. Musselroe Wind Farm replicated the structural finding, spending only 25–30% of its time within the theoretical generating boundary required for FCAS participation<sup>14</sup>.

The trade-off generalises beyond wind. Lake Bonney BESS achieved 88% enablement in Raise 6 Seconds during high-price intervals but only 24–29% in Raise Regulation, because regulation requires sustained energy throughput that competes with the contingency reserve<sup>15</sup>. The Wallgrove Battery's 50 MW nameplate becomes 24 MW for energy and regulating raise after 26 MW of contingency-raise headroom is reserved<sup>16,17</sup>. In grid-forming batteries the same headroom logic applies to inertia: as the active power setpoint approaches 1 pu, the inertial response diminishes because there is insufficient capacity for a complete response<sup>3,18</sup>. The Liddell BESS was therefore deliberately oversized and operated at 86% of maximum capacity to reserve overcurrent for inertial response<sup>19</sup>.

Sibling event evidence makes clear that this headroom trade-off interacts with adjacent failure modes — inverter current ceilings<sup>20</sup>, plant-controller minimum enablement floors<sup>21,22</sup>, and the forecast-skill ceiling that determines whether headroom can be offered with confidence<sup>23</sup>. The trade-off is structural; the *severity* depends on forecast quality, market design, and contract structure.

### 3.2 Photon and current allocation: the multi-junction PV trade-off

In tandem and multi-junction photovoltaic devices, the binding resource is the solar spectrum, which can be split between sub-cells in only one way at a time. Cluster [c1141] (sub-optimal component constrains whole-system performance) and cluster [c1136] (electrical vs optical in interlayers) capture this from the device-architecture perspective; cluster [c966] (incompatible processing conditions) captures it from the manufacturing perspective.

In ACAP's GaAs/Si four-terminal tandem, silicon cell efficiency dropped from 20.3% standalone to 3.75% in tandem because higher-energy photons were absorbed in the GaAs layer and parasitic losses in the GaAs rear reflector reduced silicon current from 40.2 to 8.3 mA/cm²<sup>5</sup>. In a three-junction InGaP/GaAs/Si Smart Stack, the silicon bottom cell limited overall performance through optical losses<sup>8</sup>. UNSW's GaAsP/SiGe project found the SiGe bottom cell current was the binding constraint, and recommended prioritising bottom-cell current over top-cell improvements because of the current-matching requirement of two-terminal tandems<sup>24</sup>.

The same trade-off appears in interlayer design. Transparent conducting oxides such as ITO improve electrical conductivity but cause parasitic absorption in the red wavelengths<sup>25,26</sup>; introducing conductive metals or particles into transparent contact adhesives raises conductivity but degrades transparency<sup>27</sup>. CdS as an electron transport layer has been used in high-efficiency antimony selenide cells but causes parasitic absorption that reduces light reaching the absorber<sup>28</sup>. The optical-electrical coupling means a single material cannot simultaneously be a perfect conductor and a perfect window.

Manufacturing integration sharpens the trade-off further. Hydrogenation processes that improve cell efficiency cause copper contacts to peel from silicon because of thermal expansion mismatch<sup>29</sup>; high-temperature firing required for screen-printed silver contacts degrades polysilicon passivation contacts<sup>30</sup>; black silicon textures that minimise reflection are incompatible with standard metal contact processes, forcing the industry to deploy "grey silicon" with moderate reflectance instead<sup>31,32</sup>. Each integration step asks two layers or two processes to satisfy mutually incompatible requirements through a single fabrication sequence.

### 3.3 Kinetics versus thermodynamics in chemical processes

Cluster [c708] gathers cases where reaction kinetics demand operating conditions that conflict with thermodynamic optima. The mechanism is fundamental: many industrially important reactions are exothermic with negative entropy change, so equilibrium yield is favoured at low temperature and high pressure, while reaction rate is favoured at high temperature.

Ammonia synthesis is the textbook case. CSIRO's hydrogen-to-ammonia project documented that the exothermic reaction is thermodynamically favoured at low temperature but slow kinetics force operation at 400–500°C, where equilibrium yield falls; at 450°C equilibrium ammonia content drops from 22% at 150 bar to 6.5% at 30 bar<sup>9,33</sup>. Calix's ZESTY direct-reduced-iron process exhibits the same pattern: the H₂-iron oxide reaction is endothermic, so complete substitution of methane with hydrogen requires additional heating that the existing CO-iron oxide reaction would otherwise provide<sup>34</sup>. Cavendish's ammonia cracking work notes that decomposition is endothermic and consumes 35% of the hydrogen's usable energy content<sup>35</sup>; in-situ cracking on the SOFC anode creates thermal gradients that stress materials<sup>36</sup>.

The trade-off extends to receiver design. Central tower CSP receivers achieve only ~90% efficiency because of peak flux limits imposed by molten salt and materials constraints<sup>37</sup>. Pursuing supercritical CO₂ Brayton cycles above 700°C raises capital cost of thermal storage<sup>38</sup>. In phosphorus removal from end-of-life PV silicon, vacuum evaporation removed 70–90% of phosphorus but with proportional silicon loss — only 50% of initial silicon mass was recoverable at 10⁻³ atm<sup>39</sup>.

These trade-offs are time-invariant. They will bind any future project with the same chemistry, regardless of market or regulatory context.

### 3.4 Reactive-real power and inverter capacity allocation

Cluster [c543] and the related clusters [c1107] and [c1181] address a coupling specific to inverter-based resources: the inverter's apparent-power capacity is shared between active and reactive power, and reactive-power dispatch to manage voltage simultaneously consumes thermal headroom and current capacity needed for active power.

The University of Melbourne's PV-rich distribution networks study established the mechanism quantitatively: aggregate Volt-var absorption at HV feeder heads can violate transmission-distribution power factor limits<sup>40</sup>, while at lower voltages the reactive absorption increases conductor and transformer currents enough that low-capacity LV transformers become overloaded at PV penetration levels well below those that would constrain a Volt-var-free network<sup>41,42</sup>. AGL's VPP in South Australia found that high local voltage triggered reactive power responses that limited active power available for FCAS; during a large dispatch, site voltages above 250 V curtailed real power output<sup>43,44</sup>. At Mortlake, enabling reactive power prioritisation during current limiting improved reactive output but reduced active output, and the connection assessment team turned the mode off<sup>45</sup>.

Tesla's SA VPP made the binding nature of the trade-off explicit at device level: the most aggressive Volt-var curve required up to 100% of Powerwall inverter output, effectively pausing all other inverter functions including self-consumption, FCAS, and inertia services<sup>46,47</sup>. Hornsdale Power Reserve Expansion noted that the H constant chosen for inertia provision was a compromise — higher inertia raised reactive current rise and settling times beyond NER limits<sup>48</sup>. Grid-forming inverter operation at very low voltage references can deliver nominal power only by violating thermal limits<sup>49</sup>.

### 3.5 Reserved-capacity opportunity cost and value-stream stacking

Cluster [c948] in its commercial expression and clusters [c985] and [c1539] capture trade-offs where the binding constraint is not physical capacity but contractual or market-architectural exclusion. A battery cannot simultaneously bid into the energy market and reserve the same MWh for contingency raise<sup>50</sup>. A demand-response provider under a RERT contract was prohibited (in 2017–2018, given AEMO's interpretation) from bidding the same load into FCAS markets during ITT windows; a seven-day ITT could erode 60 hours of FCAS revenue<sup>12,51</sup>. The Ballarat BESS faced a precautionary 75% SoC cap that drove availability from a potential 98.92% down to 91.05%<sup>52</sup>. Wallgrove Battery operators bid availability out of the market during market suspension to avoid continuous dispatch eroding their stored energy<sup>53</sup>.

The same mechanism appears in V2G trials. Optimising EV charging to minimise marginal emissions raises financial cost relative to convenience charging<sup>54</sup>; day-ahead and real-time market participation are mutually exclusive when both require bulk energy shifting<sup>55</sup>. Project Symphony confirmed that each DER asset can only be controlled for one market service at a time, even when the underlying facility could deliver multiple<sup>56</sup>.

This sub-pattern is partly time-variant: market-rule reform can change which combinations are permissible. The 2024 ARENA Vehicle-to-Grid insights report observed that V2G was not legally allowed to bid into raise regulation FCAS during the REVS trial because of battery wear concerns<sup>57</sup> — a constraint whose rationale (cycling cost) is partly physical and partly contractual.

### 3.6 Stakeholder-utility and regulatory-design trade-offs

Clusters [c1539], [c1104], [c1370], and [c1570] extend the mechanism to design trade-offs in markets, regulatory frameworks, and customer-facing systems. Here the binding resource is comprehensibility, administrative capacity, or stakeholder utility itself.

The ANU machine-learning solar forecasting project found that utilities for different market participants are in direct competition: a single performance measure cannot satisfy generators, networks, and system operators simultaneously<sup>58</sup>. A market-operator-optimal forecast minimises maximum deviation, while a generator-optimal forecast minimises mean error<sup>59</sup>. Project Edith documented the same shape of problem in dynamic network pricing: highly localised dynamic prices reflect physical conditions accurately but fragment the customer experience, while zonally averaged tariffs are accessible but obscure local scarcity<sup>10,60</sup>. Targeted rollouts addressing fairness concerns sacrifice the broader incentive value of full-network signals<sup>11</sup>. The DEIP CER Data Exchange co-design workshop noted that documenting data-sharing arrangements in a guidance note offers flexibility but weak enforcement, while NER prescription gives certainty but resists timely amendment<sup>61</sup>.

Customer compensation in DOE-style schemes faces the same shape: an overly complex compensation system erodes user trust through poor experience, while an overly simplified one erodes trust through inaccuracy<sup>62</sup>. The Advisian wind/solar forecasting project found that biasing forecasts to assist frequency stabilisation conflicts with the project objective of pure forecast accuracy<sup>63</sup>.

## 4. Cross-Cutting Observations

Three patterns emerge when the sub-patterns are read together.

**The trade-off frontier is rarely linear, and the steepness of the frontier determines whether the project is investible.** In FCAS-from-wind, the curtailment-to-FCAS ratio at Hornsdale was approximately 1.5:1 in MW terms (18.7 MW curtailment for 12.4 MW dispatch capacity)<sup>1</sup>, and the resulting net loss was the predictable consequence of a steep frontier rather than a project failure. In tandem solar, the current-matching constraint creates a particularly punishing frontier where a 10% mismatch between sub-cells can cost more than 10% of total efficiency<sup>5</sup>. In ammonia synthesis, the equilibrium-vs-rate frontier is steep enough that conventional Haber-Bosch's recycle-loop architecture exists precisely because no single-pass operating point is economic<sup>33</sup>. Steepness, not the existence of the trade-off, is the investible variable.

**Trade-offs propagate into adjacent failure modes when treated as if they were engineering oversights.** The event-sibling evidence shows this clearly. When FCAS-trapezium stranding (a direct expression of the headroom trade-off) is treated as a control architecture issue, plant-controller minimum-enablement floors become a separate failure mode at clusters [c581]<sup>21,22</sup>. When inverter current ceilings (a direct expression of the kVA trade-off) are treated as an inverter design issue rather than a trade-off, they appear as their own failure mode under inverter-based-resource grid interaction<sup>20</sup>. The temptation to engineer around a structural trade-off generally moves the failure to an adjacent surface rather than eliminating it.

**The salience of certain sub-patterns has shifted with technology and market state.** The FCAS-headroom sub-pattern was acutely salient in 2017–2018 when the Hornsdale, Musselroe, ESCRI and Lake Bonney trials were running, and remains salient today, but the *quantitative* parameters have shifted: GFM batteries with VMM now provide synthetic inertia using headroom that is partly compensated by separate procurement<sup>48</sup>; the 5-minute settlement transition (2021) altered the energy-vs-FCAS arbitrage<sup>15</sup>. By contrast, the kinetics-vs-thermodynamics sub-pattern in ammonia and DRI chemistry is essentially unchanged across the 2018–2025 period — Calix in 2024 faces the same H₂-iron-oxide endothermicity constraint that CSIRO articulated in 2018<sup>34,9</sup>. The PV multi-junction sub-pattern shows steady improvement at the Pareto frontier (record efficiencies climb) without changing the underlying coupling.

## 5. Project- and Event-Level Context

Several projects in the corpus illustrate that multiple sub-patterns of this archetype can coexist within a single asset. Hornsdale Power Reserve Expansion shows the headroom trade-off (inertia vs energy)<sup>3,18</sup>, the inverter-capacity trade-off (real vs reactive in the H-constant tuning)<sup>48</sup>, and the modular-versus-fixed scalability trade-off in synchronous condenser comparison<sup>64</sup>. Musselroe Wind Farm shows headroom trade-off, plant-controller floor effects<sup>22</sup>, and reactive-vs-active trapezium constraints<sup>14</sup>. Lake Bonney BESS shows reservation-for-FCAS opportunity cost<sup>15</sup>, AEMO-imposed islanding constraints reducing the operating envelope<sup>65</sup>, and warranty-throughput-as-binding-constraint reducing arbitrage<sup>66</sup>.

The event-sibling structure reveals that when the coupled-objective trade-off is the assigned parent, the most common adjacent failure modes are: control architecture and control-loop failure (when plant controllers cannot navigate the trade-off in real time), inverter-based-resource grid-interaction failure (when the trade-off interacts with weak-grid dynamics), price-signal distortion (when the trade-off is mediated by a market price that does not reflect the physical cost), and forecast-skill ceiling (when the trade-off depends on operating data that is itself uncertain). This co-location is informative for portfolio management: a project flagged for coupled-objective trade-off risk is statistically more likely to encounter one of these adjacent modes during commissioning or early operation.

## 6. Temporal Trajectory

The evidence dates split into three eras. The 2010–2014 records are dominated by physical-trade-off cases in solar PV materials, CSP receiver design, and early FCAS thinking. The 2017–2021 records are dominated by FCAS, inverter, and DER trade-offs as the National Electricity Market began absorbing variable renewables and storage at scale. The 2022–2025 records add hydrogen, iron-and-steel chemistry, and dynamic-tariff/regulatory design trade-offs, plus advanced inverter (GFM) trade-offs.

The mechanism class is not weakening over time. If anything, it is becoming more visible because the portfolio is increasingly tackling problems whose value proposition depends on co-optimisation. A 2012 PV-cell project could in principle be evaluated as a single-objective efficiency problem; a 2024 GFM battery project must be evaluated against simultaneous obligations to provide energy, FCAS, system strength, and inertia. The binding-resource problem is structurally more central to the current investment portfolio than to the historical one.

Specific time-bound claims: the FCAS-RERT mutual-exclusion under NER 3.20.3(j)<sup>12,51</sup> was salient in 2017–2018 and may have been moderated by subsequent market reforms. The dynamic-tariff design trade-offs<sup>10,11,60</sup> are actively being resolved in Project Edith and DEIP processes through 2024–2026. The kinetic-thermodynamic trade-offs in ammonia and DRI chemistry are time-invariant.

## 7. Mitigation Patterns

Mitigations observed in the corpus fall into four categories.

*Move the trade-off to a different resource.* Adding a battery alongside a wind farm transfers the FCAS headroom problem from the wind farm's available power to the battery's reserved capacity — solving the wind farm's curtailment problem at the cost of adding new capital. Oversizing GFM inverters at Liddell to 86% operating capacity converts a real-time trade-off into a capex trade-off<sup>19</sup>. These mitigations are effective when the moved-to resource is cheaper than the original; they fail when capital cost erodes the business case.

*Accept the trade-off and price it.* Hornsdale 2's PPA provider absorbed the financial impact of FCAS curtailment because the impacts were small enough given the trial circumstances<sup>67</sup>. Reactive-power prioritisation can be enabled or disabled as a matter of grid-code negotiation rather than design<sup>45</sup>. This mitigation is effective when the counterparty has flexibility; it fails when contracts are rigid.

*Co-optimise across the frontier.* AEMO's MASS verification framework, Lake Bonney's later optimisation modules<sup>66</sup>, and bid-stacking algorithms in V2G all attempt to find the operating point on the trade-off frontier that maximises total value. This requires accurate forecasts, which themselves face a forecast-skill ceiling<sup>23</sup>. The combination — coupled-objective trade-off plus forecast uncertainty — is harder to manage than either alone.

*Invest in better resources.* Synthetic inertia with VMM partially decouples inertia from energy headroom<sup>3</sup>. Tandem-cell research aims to widen the photon-allocation frontier through better band engineering<sup>24,25</sup>. Process intensification in ammonia synthesis (membrane reactors) shifts the equilibrium-rate frontier<sup>33,68</sup>. These are R&D-grade mitigations whose payoff is structural but slow.

The general lesson is that mitigation effectiveness is sub-pattern-specific. Headroom trade-offs respond well to capital additions; chemistry trade-offs respond only to fundamental research; market-architectural trade-offs respond to rule reform; design trade-offs around comprehensibility require iterative co-design.

## 8. Implications for Portfolio Decisions

A portfolio manager who recognises this archetype as pervasive should change three decision points.

*At funding.* Business cases should be required to identify the binding resource that two or more project objectives share, and to estimate the slope of the trade-off frontier at the proposed operating point. A project that proposes to deliver simultaneous FCAS and energy revenue, or simultaneous inertia and energy arbitrage, or simultaneous high efficiency and high stability in a tandem cell, should present a co-optimisation analysis rather than two separate revenue projections. Hornsdale's net trial loss of approximately $59,000 (FCAS revenue minus curtailed energy value)<sup>13</sup> is a forecastable outcome from the headroom mechanics alone, not a surprising result.

*At milestone review.* When a project reports underperformance against a single metric, the review should ask whether the metric is one side of a coupled trade-off whose other side is being delivered. Lake Bonney's energy arbitrage shortfall<sup>15</sup>, ESCRI-SA's similar shortfall<sup>69</sup>, and several FCAS trial losses are not failures against a complete metric set; they are predictable expressions of the headroom trade-off. Reviewing both metrics together changes the assessment.

*At knowledge sharing.* The corpus contains substantial overlap in how different projects rediscover the same shape of trade-off. A coordinated knowledge product on the binding-resource analysis pattern — what the resource is, how to estimate the frontier slope, when capital additions move the trade-off versus eliminate it — would prevent the third and fourth project in a category from spending discovery effort on a known structure. The Hornsdale-2, Musselroe, Lake Bonney and ESCRI experiences in 2017–2021 collectively converged on a small set of conclusions that could have been transferred forward to GFM battery projects in 2022–2025 with less duplicated effort.

For the time-variant sub-patterns specifically, the portfolio should acknowledge that older evidence may no longer reflect current rule sets. The FCAS-RERT mutual-exclusion of 2017–2018 may not bind today; the NER reactive-current limits cited at HPRX in 2019 may be different now. Where a current project's business case rests on a regulatory state that has since shifted, due diligence should include current-rule confirmation rather than reliance on the older record.

## 9. Open Questions and Evidence Gaps

Several sub-patterns are thinly evidenced and warrant attention.

The customer-trust trade-off in DOE-style schemes<sup>62</sup> rests on only four records. As DOEs roll out at scale through 2026 and beyond, evidence will accumulate quickly, but the corpus today does not establish whether the simplification-vs-accuracy frontier is steep or shallow in customer perception, and so does not determine which side of the frontier projects should default to.

The geothermal coupled-objective trade-off<sup>70,71</sup> has only three records, all from a now-closed project corpus. The corpus does not tell us whether geothermal trade-offs (permeability vs short-circuiting; reduction vs HER selectivity) generalise to other deep-resource settings.

The electrolyser-grid-connection trade-off<sup>72,73</sup> — between behind-the-meter DC-to-DC efficiency and direct-grid capacity factor — was identified as unresolved at the close of two hydrogen projects. Whether subsequent FEED studies (e.g. CQ-H2, Gibson Island, Lochard) have resolved this is not visible in the corpus.

More generally, the corpus rarely provides the *quantitative slope* of the trade-off frontiers it documents. We know that FCAS headroom requires curtailment, but the dollar-cost of one MWh of energy foregone per MW-hour of FCAS provided varies by project, market state, and time of day, and is often calculated post-hoc rather than in design. Asking projects to surface this slope explicitly, both pre-commitment and post-operation, would substantially improve the portfolio's ability to anticipate where the next coupled-objective trade-off will bind.

A final gap: the corpus contains few records where the trade-off was anticipated, characterised, and successfully navigated. The visible cases tend to be those where the trade-off bound harder than expected. There is likely a selection bias in knowledge-sharing reports toward post-hoc trade-off recognition rather than ex-ante design; deliberate solicitation of "trade-off characterisation" deliverables in milestone reporting would help close this gap.
