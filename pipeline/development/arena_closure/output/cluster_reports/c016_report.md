# FCAS Revenue Compression and the Battery Business Case: A Portfolio Review

## The mechanism in plain language

A recurring failure pattern across ARENA's storage and ancillary-services portfolio is what might be called the "first-mover premium decay" problem in Frequency Control Ancillary Services (FCAS) markets. Battery and other fast-response projects have routinely been justified to investors and to ARENA on financial models that assume continuation — or only modest decline — of the high FCAS prices observed in the early years of operation. In practice, those high prices have been a transient artefact of thin markets with few qualified providers. As subsequent batteries enter, prices compress sharply, and the revenue stack that underwrote the original investment thins out from the bottom up. The Hornsdale Power Reserve effect — a single 100 MW battery taking 55% of South Australia's FCAS market and crashing prices by roughly 90% in early 2018 [Networks Renewed/ARENA-DLV-0954-0038] — is the canonical illustration: the same asset that demonstrated FCAS commercial viability simultaneously demonstrated how easily it could be eroded.

This matters disproportionately for ARENA because the Agency's mandate sits at exactly the project stage where this risk is invisible. ARENA funds first-of-kind and early-mover deployments whose business cases lean heavily on services revenues that have not yet been competed away. The cluster contains 59 records from 23 projects across 11 categories — battery storage, hybrid systems, V2G, demand response, pumped hydro, distributed energy resources, system security, even concentrated solar thermal — meaning the mechanism is not a battery-specific quirk but a structural feature of how shallow ancillary-service markets interact with successive waves of ARENA-style demonstration funding.

## The causal pathway

The vulnerability arises from a specific market structure. FCAS markets in the NEM are small in absolute terms — roughly 210–220 MW for regulation and 350–450 MW for contingency [c016/ARENA-DLV-1086-0057] — and the marginal cost of supply from a fast-response battery is essentially zero once the asset exists. A small number of projects can saturate the market, and the next entrant is a price-taker into a clearing price that its own bid drives down. The proximate failure manifests as a step-change between operating year one and year two: revenues that anchored the investment case fall sharply and persistently, not because of asset underperformance but because the market has restructured around the new entrants. Gannawarra Energy Storage System (GESS) is the cleanest empirical example — FCAS revenue collapsed from roughly $500,000/month in its first operating year to roughly $150,000/month in its second, a ~70% reduction with no change in the asset itself [c016/ARENA-DLV-0541-0018, c016/ARENA-DLV-0539-0007].

What distinguishes this mechanism from adjacent ones is the source of the compression. It is not driven primarily by the renewables-penetration effects that raise *requirement* for FCAS (cluster c650), nor by reserved-capacity tradeoffs within an asset (c948), nor by regulatory rule changes (c903) — though all of these can compound it. The core dynamic is endogenous: success of the deployment model creates the conditions under which subsequent deployments cannot replicate the original returns. It is, in effect, a tragedy-of-the-commons in a finite revenue pool, with each new entrant rationally optimising against a price curve that their collective entry destroys.

## Manifestation across the portfolio

The pattern appears at three distinguishable points in the project lifecycle.

**At feasibility and application stage**, it shows up as wide divergence in FCAS price assumptions between applicants. ARENA's own portfolio review noted that some applicants assumed current high FCAS prices would persist while others applied aggressive decay curves [c016/ARENA-DLV-0402-0060]. The AECOM analysis commissioned by ARENA, which modelled exponential decline to 10% of current FCAS prices by 2040, was being used by some applicants (notably the ANU community-battery work [Community Models for Deploying and Operating DER, c016/ARENA-DLV-0597-0046, c016/ARENA-DLV-0401-0048]) but evidently not by others. One LSBS portfolio review explicitly applied a -10% per annum de-escalator [c016/ARENA-DLV-0784-0076], implying that even at the time of review the variance in applicant practice was material. This matters because the bankability gap — projects requiring FCAS revenues but unable to bank them [c016/ARENA-DLV-0254-0016, c016/ARENA-DLV-0556-0009, c016/ARENA-DLV-0651-0041] — has been consistently bridged by ARENA grant funding, transferring the price-trajectory risk implicitly onto the public balance sheet.

**At early-operations stage**, it shows up as the GESS-style year-two surprise. The Wallgrove battery experienced this in a more attenuated form: regulation FCAS revenue declined from $2.3M (H1 2022) to $1.2M (H2 2023), though aggregate revenues across all markets stayed relatively stable as the operator restacked into other streams [TransGrid Wallgrove Battery, c016/ARENA-DLV-0978-0043]. ESCRI/Dalrymple in South Australia reported FCAS forming over 90% of competitive market revenue in its first six months — a concentration that the project itself flagged as a risk for the next wave of batteries [Energy Storage for Commercial Renewable Integration Phase 2, c016/ARENA-DLV-0480-0070, c016/ARENA-DLV-0479-0050]. Even non-battery ancillary-service plays were affected: United Energy's voltage-controlled FCAS trial, designed to monetise its Dynamic Voltage Management System, found that R5M revenues had collapsed across 2017–2020 — Q3 revenues falling from ~$26,600 (2018) to $539 (2020) — to the point where projected revenue fell below the daily maintenance cost threshold [United Energy Voltage Controlled Frequency Regulation System, c016/ARENA-DLV-1030-0041, c016/ARENA-DLV-1030-0045, c016/ARENA-DLV-1030-0050].

**At late or post-deployment stage**, the mechanism manifests as commercial models that simply did not survive contact with the market. The United Energy Low Voltage Battery Trial is the most striking case: by the time the pole-mounted distributed battery fleet was ready for FCAS participation, contingency prices had compressed to the point where the retail partner advised it was no longer economic to register at all [United Energy Low Voltage Battery Trial, c016/ARENA-DLV-1008-0002, c016/ARENA-DLV-1008-0039, c016/ARENA-DLV-1008-0003]. The trial proved technical feasibility but lost the commercial window — a pattern with uncomfortable echoes of merchant generation projects that come online into a saturated market. Similarly, Hornsdale Wind Farm Stage 2 found that the regulation FCAS opportunity for additional wind providers had already been compressed by the entry of HPR and HDWF2 itself [Hornsdale Wind Farm Stage 2 FCAS Trial, c016/ARENA-DLV-0742-0038].

A sub-pattern worth flagging is the **adjacent-market displacement effect**. As FCAS compresses in primary markets, projects look to secondary streams — SRAS, contingency 6-second/60-second/5-minute markets, energy arbitrage, network leasing. Each of these secondary streams is itself thin and subject to the same compression dynamic. The ESCRI BESS turned to contingency FCAS as regulation eroded; GESS registered for contingency markets in its third year only to face Victorian Big Battery competition [Gannawarra/c016/ARENA-DLV-0539-0012]. SRAS was identified as a potential future revenue stream for several projects but assessed as economically unattractive due to competition from less capital-intensive incumbents [c016/ARENA-DLV-0556-0091]. The pattern is thus not "FCAS revenue falls" but rather "each successive ancillary-service revenue pool is competed away in turn", with cycle time accelerating.

## Project-level context

The project context illuminates *why* certain projects bore this risk acutely. GESS, with $22.7M of ARENA funding against a $41.2M project value, was a co-located battery+solar deployment whose merchant exposure was substantial; its operator was sophisticated, and the FCAS revenue collapse was not a planning failure but a market reality the project documented carefully across reporting periods. ESCRI Phase 2, similarly, was a network-adjacent battery (ElectraNet) whose primary value was system-security oriented, but whose competitive market revenues to defray cost depended on the same FCAS pool. The United Energy LV trial is the most cautionary case from a portfolio-design perspective: at $4M ARENA funding into a $14.4M trial whose commercial model rested on retail-partner FCAS participation, the project was structurally vulnerable to the precise mechanism that ultimately materialised, and the cascade of upstream manufacturing/installation delays [event EVT-0002] meant the project entered the FCAS market window after it had effectively closed.

A different lens applies to the feasibility-study projects — EnergyAustralia PHES, Shoalhaven, the ANU community models work. Here, FCAS compression entered the projects as a *finding* rather than a project-level failure: studies that initially assumed FCAS as a meaningful revenue line revised that assumption downward during the analysis itself, with consequences for the apparent commercial viability of the underlying technology [c016/ARENA-DLV-0506-0045, c016/ARENA-DLV-0899-0083]. In several cases this contributed to projects not progressing to deployment. From a portfolio-risk standpoint, this is arguably a healthier outcome: the feasibility funding surfaced the vulnerability before deployment capital was committed.

## Event-level context

The event-sibling structure shows that FCAS compression rarely sits alone. In the Ballarat BESS project, the cluster record on FCAS uncertainty sits alongside sibling records [c650/ARENA-DLV-0332-0056, c650/ARENA-DLV-0334-0032] showing that high renewables penetration was simultaneously suppressing both energy and FCAS prices — a compounding rather than offsetting dynamic. In the United Energy LV trial, the FCAS-failure record sits in an event with cascading delay records [c648/ARENA-DLV-1005-0086, c565/ARENA-DLV-1005-0101] and a regulatory-rule-change record [c903/ARENA-DLV-1008-0032]: the proximate cause of commercial failure was FCAS compression, but the underlying vulnerability was a project schedule that exhausted the commercial window. The Rheem hot-water VPP shows FCAS dilution from new residential battery entrants paired with regulatory uncertainty over MASS rules [c682/ARENA-DLV-1378-0019] — the two risks reinforce each other because rule changes can themselves accelerate competitive entry. Reading across these event clusters, the mechanism is best understood as a *vulnerability* that is triggered or amplified by adjacent failures — schedule slippage, regulatory shifts, renewables-driven energy-price suppression — rather than as a free-standing failure mode.

## Mitigations observed

Several mitigation strategies appear across the records, with varying success.

*Conservative price assumptions in evaluation*. The -10% de-escalator practice [c016/ARENA-DLV-0784-0076] and the AECOM exponential-decline curve [c016/ARENA-DLV-0597-0046] represent attempts to discipline applicant projections. Implementation appears uneven across the portfolio.

*Revenue stacking and rotation*. Wallgrove's relatively stable aggregate revenues despite regulation FCAS decline [c016/ARENA-DLV-0978-0043] suggest that operators with mature trading capability can rotate between revenue streams, partially offsetting compression in any single one. This requires sophisticated optimisation infrastructure — the ESCRI sibling records [event EVT-0019] note that AGL was still developing arbitrage-vs-FCAS optimisation modules as five-minute settlement approached.

*Network-service contracting*. Several records emphasise that network-benefit revenues, where contractable on long-term terms, provide a hedge against merchant FCAS volatility — though the ARENA review noted that networks themselves are uncertain about their ability to negotiate or sustain such leases [c016/ARENA-DLV-0402-0074]. This is the implicit logic behind ESCRI and Wallgrove's network-host structures.

*Direct facilitation*. The portfolio-level review [c016/ARENA-DLV-0556-0047] candidly observes that most grid-scale BESS to date has required direct government facilitation. This is itself a mitigation, but one that does not scale.

*Hedging product development*. The Renewable Energy Hub Marketplace project [c016/ARENA-DLV-0856-0046] is notable as a project that takes FCAS compression as a given and attempts to build the financial-product infrastructure (hedge contracts) that would let battery operators monetise firming and arbitrage revenues more reliably. This is the most structurally responsive mitigation in the corpus — addressing the underlying bankability problem rather than the symptom.

## Implications for portfolio decisions

For a portfolio manager, three concrete implications follow.

**At funding-decision time**, FCAS revenue assumptions in applicant business cases should be treated as a primary risk parameter, not a secondary financial input. A standardised stress-test — for example, applying the AECOM-style decline curve and asking whether the project remains viable — would quickly separate projects whose case is robust from those that are essentially a bet that the applicant will be early enough to capture the compression premium. Where the latter is the case, that should be made explicit. The corpus suggests that ARENA has implicitly been funding compression-premium bets without always pricing them as such.

**At milestone-review time**, year-one FCAS performance is a leading indicator, not a result. Strong year-one numbers should trigger heightened scrutiny of year-two projections rather than reassurance, because year-one returns reflect the market state *before* the funded project (and its successors in the pipeline) have changed it. The GESS reporting cadence [event EVT-0004, event EVT-0008] is a model: granular tracking of FCAS revenue evolution by half-year, with explicit attribution to market-share and competitor-entry dynamics.

**At handover and knowledge-sharing time**, the corpus would benefit from a standardised retrospective on the gap between business-case FCAS revenue and realised FCAS revenue, with attribution between price compression, dispatch performance, and market-design changes. Several projects produce this implicitly; few produce it in a comparable form.

**At programme design level**, ARENA may want to consider whether sequential funding of similar batteries into the same FCAS market constitutes — viewed from outside — a form of subsidised cannibalisation. If the public benefit of project N+1 is partly offset by the revenue erosion it imposes on already-funded projects 1 through N, the marginal-benefit calculation is not what it appears.

## Open questions and evidence gaps

The corpus is rich on the *symptom* but thin on several aspects of the underlying dynamic.

First, there is essentially no empirical work in the corpus on the *floor* of FCAS price compression — at what level prices stabilise once the market is saturated, and whether that floor reflects long-run marginal cost of provision or some other equilibrium. The ANU community-battery projects use the AECOM decline-to-10% curve, but the empirical basis for that floor is unclear.

Second, the interaction between FCAS compression and the introduction of Primary Frequency Response, the 1-second/2-second contingency markets, and broader frequency-control rule reforms is acknowledged [c016/ARENA-DLV-0332-0060, c016/ARENA-DLV-0332-0061] but not quantified. Rule changes can both compress (by mandating provision) and expand (by creating new product markets) the available revenue pool; the corpus does not resolve which dominates.

Third, the V2G records [c016/ARENA-DLV-0031-0012, c016/ARENA-DLV-0031-0037, c016/ARENA-DLV-0950-0097] anticipate massive future EV-driven FCAS supply but contain no empirical data on whether this is materialising — it is forward-looking concern rather than observed phenomenon.

Fourth, the corpus contains very little on whether projects that survived FCAS compression did so through superior trading, hardware capability (e.g. faster response), site-specific advantages, or simply scale — i.e. what differentiates resilient from fragile FCAS revenue strategies. Wallgrove's relative stability hints at trading sophistication but is not isolated as a causal factor.

Filling these gaps — in particular through a coordinated cross-project retrospective on realised vs. projected FCAS revenues, indexed to market-share and competitor-entry timelines — would substantially sharpen the portfolio's ability to evaluate the next wave of storage and ancillary-service applications.
