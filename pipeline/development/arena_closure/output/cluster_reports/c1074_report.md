# Conservative Third-Party Engineering Bias Toward Over-Specification

## A pattern of asymmetric incentives at the technical-advisory layer

Across ARENA's portfolio, there is a recurring failure mode that lives not in the technology being funded, nor in the proponent organisation, but in the *advisory layer* between them — the independent engineers, certification consultants, transmission network service providers (TNSPs), specifying authorities, and equipment vendors whose sign-off is required to move a project forward. The mechanism is straightforward and economically rational: these third parties bear concentrated downside risk if a design they signed off on fails in the field, but capture none of the upside if the design is optimised. The rational response is to over-specify — to apply the most conservative interpretation of every standard, to assume the worst plausible loading or operating condition, to default to the most familiar (and typically heaviest, largest, costliest) configuration. The proponent absorbs the resulting cost inflation, sometimes catastrophically.

This archetype appears in seven distinct projects spanning five ARENA categories — battery storage, concentrated solar thermal, solar PV, system security, and renewables for industry. That breadth is itself a finding: this is not a quirk of one technology family or one regulatory regime. It is a structural feature of how technical risk is allocated whenever a novel or non-standard design must pass through an established advisory function. For a portfolio whose mandate is precisely to fund the novel and non-standard, the mechanism is endemic rather than incidental.

## The causal pathway

The vulnerability arises wherever three conditions coincide. First, a proponent is pursuing a design that diverges — sometimes mildly, sometimes radically — from incumbent practice. Second, that design must be validated, certified, connected, or specified by an independent technical party whose professional liability attaches to the sign-off. Third, the standards or norms available to that party were calibrated against incumbent technology and offer no defensible margin for optimisation toward the new design.

Under these conditions, the third party faces a sharply asymmetric payoff: a conservative recommendation produces no career or commercial reward but eliminates liability; an optimised recommendation produces no fee uplift but creates a defensible target if anything goes wrong later. The proximate failure is over-specification — heavier structures, larger transformers, oversized boilers, tighter harmonic allocations, evaporator tubes built to superheater spec — and the cost shows up on the proponent's capex line.

What distinguishes this mechanism from adjacent ones is important. It is *not* the same as risk-averse adoption delay (cluster c536), where the conservatism slows or blocks the decision to adopt at all; here the project proceeds, but with a fattened cost base. It is *not* the same as inadequate measurement infrastructure (c894), though the two often co-occur — measurement gaps *enable* the conservative bias by removing the empirical basis for arguing against it. And it is not generic engineering conservatism: it is specifically conservatism produced by *misaligned incentives at the advisory interface*, which means the mitigation set is institutional rather than technical.

## Manifestation across the portfolio

The clearest articulation comes from the 5B Maverick project [c1074/ARENA-DLV-1092-0020], where the proponent named the mechanism in essentially economic terms: independent wind tunnel consultants have no upside for optimisation and material downside for any field failure traceable to deviation from norms. The 5B case is also the most fully documented at event level. The wind certification difficulty was compounded by measurement infrastructure designed for high-rise buildings rather than ground-mounted low-load PV arrays [ARENA-DLV-1092-0021], which produced a poor signal-to-noise ratio that *itself* fed back into conservative load assumptions — when the measurement is noisy, the defensible response is to take the upper envelope. 5B's workaround was a multi-laboratory round robin to triangulate true loads [ARENA-DLV-0195-0058], described as slow and expensive. The same event cluster captures the broader problem of asking incumbent-trained engineers to assess novel geometries [ARENA-DLV-0195-0057].

In the network and grid-connection space, the AGL Liddell BESS project [Liddell Large Scale Battery] shows the mechanism operating through the regulatory-standards interface rather than the consultancy interface. The TNSP applied its NER-regulated asset standards to a privately owned connection substation [c1074/ARENA-DLV-1105-0017], inflating footprint and cost, and was unwilling to deviate. A sibling record [ARENA-DLV-1105-0018] shows the same pattern producing a specific equipment-level penalty: oversized current transformers were specified instead of relying on modern relay algorithms to handle CT saturation, delaying switchboard manufacture. This is the same archetype with different actors — the TNSP plays the role the wind consultant plays for 5B, and the asymmetry is identical: deviating from regulated asset standards exposes the TNSP to liability with no offsetting reward.

In concentrated solar thermal, the Collinsville hybrid project [Conversion of Coal to Hybrid Solar Thermal/Gas] shows the mechanism inside the EPC supply chain. The vendor (Novatec) specified evaporator tubes to the same standard as superheater tubes [c1074/ARENA-DLV-1429-0004], which contributed materially to a solar field cost overrun. The event context is sobering: the EPC tender came in 84% above the May 2013 budget estimate [ARENA-DLV-1428-0003], with currency moves explaining roughly 16.5% [ARENA-DLV-1428-0004] and the residual driven by solar-field-specific cost inflation [ARENA-DLV-1428-0005]. Over-specification was one of several contributors, but it sat inside a stack that ultimately killed project feasibility. The same project's design-proliferation problem [ARENA-DLV-1428-0006] is in fact the same evaporator-tube issue viewed through a different cluster lens, which is itself instructive — over-specification and design proliferation tend to coincide because conservative spec choices propagate as bespoke variants.

The CSIRO heliostat R&D project [Novel Concepts for Low Cost Small Heliostats] reveals a slightly different manifestation: site-specific uncertainty drives conservative costings and safety factors as a *default* posture pending full site characterisation [c1074/ARENA-DLV-1310-0022]. The event sibling [ARENA-DLV-1310-0032] explains why this becomes structural: full geotechnical and wind characterisation is expensive and impractical at early project stages, so conservative assumptions are locked into commercial offers. The advisor's incentive asymmetry here is reinforced by genuine information scarcity.

In system security, the Wollongong harmonic distortion project [Impact and Management of Harmonic Distortion] surfaces the mechanism at the regulatory-modelling layer [c1074/ARENA-DLV-1009-0011]: harmonic allocations to renewable generators appear systematically too stringent, with the empirical evidence being that distortion levels have *fallen* despite increased connection of harmonic sources. The event context [ARENA-DLV-1009-0012] points to modelling practices being inaccurate as well as overly stringent — a double penalty for renewable generators required to invest in mitigation that the network does not actually need.

The remaining two records show the archetype in different guises. The Renewable Energy for Process Heat study [c1074/ARENA-DLV-0869-0012] documents a sales-side variant: conventional boiler quotes are oversized for rare peaks, and when heat pump alternatives are required to match nameplate boiler capacity, the comparison is structurally biased against the renewable option. United Energy's peak-demand-deferral work [c1074/ARENA-DLV-0879-0094] is the mildest case in the cluster — really a methodological caution about not over-specifying storage deployment in high-growth areas — but it belongs because the same logic is at play: defaulting to a conservative storage sizing assumption rather than tuning to actual local growth rates produces over-specification.

## Sub-patterns

Three sub-patterns are visible. The first is *certification-interface* over-specification (5B Maverick, Collinsville evaporator tubes, heliostat safety factors) — independent engineers signing off on novel hardware. The second is *regulatory-asset* over-specification (Liddell TNSP standards, harmonic allocations) — established network operators applying incumbent rules to novel connections. The third is *commercial-comparison* over-specification (process heat boiler sizing) — incumbent quoting practices that disadvantage renewable alternatives even before formal evaluation begins. All three share the underlying asymmetry, but the leverage points for mitigation differ.

## Mitigation patterns observed

The corpus is thinner on mitigations than on the mechanism itself, which is telling. 5B's multi-laboratory round-robin [ARENA-DLV-0195-0058] is the clearest worked example: when no single advisor can be pushed off the conservative envelope, run several in parallel to characterise the envelope itself. It works but is slow and expensive. The Wollongong harmonics project implicitly proposes a different mitigation: empirical recalibration of the allocation methodology against observed network behaviour, on the basis that the underlying modelling is wrong and not merely cautious. The heliostat project points toward earlier and cheaper site characterisation as a way to collapse the uncertainty that conservative assumptions are filling.

What is conspicuously absent is any record of an effective renegotiation of the *liability allocation* itself — i.e., a contractual or institutional arrangement under which the third party is rewarded for, or at least not penalised for, optimisation. This is the underlying economic problem and the corpus suggests it is being routed around rather than solved.

## Project-level context

The funding scale across the cluster spans three orders of magnitude, from the $146k Wollongong harmonics study to the $694m Liddell BESS, and the mechanism appears at every scale. That is itself important: this is not a small-project problem driven by lean engineering teams nor a large-project problem driven by bureaucratic inertia. It is invariant to scale because it is invariant to the asymmetry that produces it.

Two project-level observations sharpen the picture. First, the projects where the mechanism produced the most material damage — Collinsville (project ultimately not feasible) and Liddell (live cost and schedule impact on a $694m build) — are projects where the third-party advisor sat on the *critical path* of capex commitment, not in an advisory side-channel. The leverage of the conservative bias scales with how directly the advisor's sign-off translates into procurement. Second, R&D-stage projects (heliostats, harmonics) tend to surface the mechanism as a *characterised problem* rather than a realised cost — they are doing the diagnostic work that deployment-stage projects pay for in capex.

## Implications for portfolio decisions

At funding-decision time, the relevant due-diligence question is not "does this design work" but "which third parties must sign off on this design, and what are their incentive structures." Projects that route around incumbent advisory infrastructure (in-house certification, vendor-integrated testing, novel measurement chains) carry a different risk profile than projects that depend on a TNSP, an independent engineer, or a standards body whose mandate predates the technology. For a portfolio whose value proposition is novelty, the latter category should expect a conservative-bias premium baked into capex estimates, and proponents who have not budgeted for it are under-budgeted.

At milestone-review time, the diagnostic signal is cost variance concentrated in line items signed off by independent advisors rather than by the proponent's engineers. The Collinsville solar field overrun [Conversion of Coal to Hybrid Solar Thermal/Gas] and the Liddell substation footprint inflation [AGL Liddell] are textbook examples — overruns that look like generic cost inflation but on inspection trace to specific specification choices made by parties outside the proponent's control. Reviewers should ask whether overruns are concentrated at advisory interfaces.

At handover and knowledge-capture time, the most valuable artefacts are not the final designs but the *deltas* between proponent-preferred and advisor-required specifications, with cost attached. These deltas are the empirical basis for any future renegotiation of standards and allocation methodologies. ARENA's portfolio is in a privileged position to assemble this evidence base across multiple projects and technologies, and the harmonics project [Impact and Management of Harmonic Distortion] is a model for what that empirical recalibration looks like.

A portfolio-level intervention worth considering is convening standards bodies, TNSPs, and certification consultants around specific technology-class evidence packages — wind loading on ground-mount PV, harmonic allocation under high inverter penetration, BESS connection asset standards — to update norms against accumulated field evidence. This is the only mitigation that addresses the mechanism at root rather than working around it.

## Open questions and evidence gaps

The corpus tells us the mechanism exists, where it appears, and roughly what it costs in specific cases. It does not tell us several things that would matter for prioritisation. We do not have a quantified distribution of conservative-bias premium by technology class — anecdotally Collinsville lost 84% to budget but we cannot decompose how much was conservative bias versus currency, scope change, or genuine market price movement. We do not have evidence on whether the bias is increasing or decreasing as renewable technologies mature; the harmonics evidence suggests allocations are still calibrated to old assumptions, but we have no comparable longitudinal data for wind loading, grid connection, or thermal specifications. We have almost no evidence on *successful* renegotiations of advisory liability — cases where a third party was persuaded or contractually structured into supporting an optimised design — which is precisely the evidence needed to design a portfolio-level mitigation. And we have no clear picture of whether Australian advisory practice is more or less conservative than international comparators; the harmonics project hints at this through Dutch, Estonian, and Irish data but does not extend the comparison to other technical domains.

Filling these gaps would not require new projects so much as more structured post-project review of the advisory-interface delta in completed builds. That artefact, assembled across the portfolio, is the substrate on which a structural rather than project-by-project response to this archetype could be built.
