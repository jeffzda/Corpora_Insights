# Hydrogen — Delivery Risk Profile

## Executive Summary

Before your first meeting about a hydrogen project, understand this: commercial viability is the make-or-break issue. The evidence shows that green hydrogen projects routinely fail to reach FID not because of technical problems, but because the economics don't work at current electrolyser costs and renewable electricity prices. The Edify Green Hydrogen Project achieved FEED completion but remained "commercially unviable" with LCOH of $26-33/kg against customer willingness to pay. The Gibson Island project faced "challenging economics" with power representing 57% of production cost. Multiple projects — CQ-H2, HVHH Phase 2, Port Augusta Solar Methanol — were discontinued after FEED due to commercial non-viability despite technical feasibility.

Regulatory gaps are the second critical risk, particularly for novel applications. The H2RESTORE project identified that "The Victorian Petroleum Act 1998 does not currently permit underground hydrogen storage," creating "project-stopping regulatory uncertainty." Queensland's Hydrogen Safety Code of Practice arrived mid-commissioning for the QUT project, adding $0.5 million in costs and a one-year delay. Technical underperformance, while present, is manageable when anticipated — the real project killers are commercial gaps and regulatory uncertainty.

For execution, plan for international supply chains with extensive compliance adaptation. The Yuri project required "significant rework of engineering deliverables from Chinese vendors...due to poor quality, non-compliance with project standards, and documentation gaps." Equipment lead times consistently exceeded vendor estimates: "Lead times communicated by hydrogen equipment suppliers during the tendering phase were overly optimistic."

## The Evidence Base

This analysis draws on 1,418 records from 81 ARENA hydrogen projects spanning 2012-2024, with the majority (824 records, 53 projects) from 2022-2024. The temporal distribution is important: 44 records carry temporal warnings indicating pre-2021 insights about costs and technology capability that may no longer reflect current conditions. However, findings about governance, coordination, stakeholder management, and design process failures are time-stable and remain relevant.

The evidence spans the full hydrogen value chain from R&D through to commercial demonstration, providing unusually rich insight into where projects actually encounter problems. Coverage includes major FEED studies (CQ-H2, ScaleH2, Gibson Island), first-of-kind demonstrations (Yuri, Denham, H2RESTORE), and early commercial deployments (Hydrogen Park Murray Valley, New Energies Service Station).

## Where Things Go Wrong

### Commercial Reality Check: The Economics Don't Add Up

Commercial and market failures dominate the adverse record set, representing 25% of all problems and showing 45% severity escalation — the highest of any failure mode. The pattern is clear: hydrogen projects can achieve technical feasibility but fail on commercial viability.

The CQ-H2 project exemplifies this dynamic. After completing an advanced Class 4 FEED study with "an optimised hydrogen production price of $7.39/kg over the first 15 years," the project was discontinued in June 2025. Despite technical maturity — the design reached Class 4 definition for both the hydrogen production facility and gas pipeline — "offtakers were generally unwilling to commit financially during the development phase." The price point, even without incorporating available government support, could not secure commercial offtake.

Similarly, the Edify Green Hydrogen Project completed FEED "under budget and within schedule" but achieved an LCOH of $26-33/kg that "proved to be restrictively high" for potential offtakers. "Extensive offtaker engagement across maritime, rail, mobility, process heat, ammonia, and green fuels sectors failed to secure any offtake agreement during the FEED period." The project faced a classic chicken-and-egg problem: "potential offtakers required price certainty and project maturity...before committing, while project development investment required offtake certainty."

The cost escalation pattern is systemic. The Edify project's "overall project budget increased from A$87 million to A$123 million, a 41% cost increase" between application and FEED completion. The Yuri project saw "the project schedule grow from 19 months to 33 months (a 14-month extension)" with corresponding cost impacts. These escalations consistently pushed projects beyond viable economics.

Even projects targeting export markets encounter commercial reality. The ATCO ScaleH2 feasibility study found that "ScaleH2's proposed 800 ktpa production capacity significantly exceeds the scale of projects that have successfully secured offtake through European hydrogen auctions," where winning projects ranged from 66-300 ktpa. The project couldn't access German or European support programs because it didn't meet location requirements, leaving it dependent on H2Global's "short delivery window" that "structurally disadvantages projects that have not yet obtained development consent."

### Regulatory Uncertainty: The Hidden Project Killer

Regulatory and approval failures account for 22% of adverse records with 29% severity escalation. The critical insight is that regulatory uncertainty — not just approval delays — kills projects by making commercial close impossible.

The H2RESTORE underground hydrogen storage project provides the starkest example: "The Victorian Petroleum Act 1998 does not currently permit underground hydrogen storage — hydrogen is not a hydrocarbon and falls outside the Act's definition of petroleum." This "fundamental regulatory gap" meant that "both the Demonstration Pilot and Commercial Facility" could not proceed because no legal approval pathway existed. The project team noted this created "project-stopping regulatory uncertainty that cannot be resolved through normal permitting engagement alone."

The timing dimension is crucial. The QUT H2Xport project was "caught off-guard" when Queensland introduced its Hydrogen Safety Code of Practice in August 2023 "while the pilot plant's gas system remained uncommissioned due to COVID-related delays." Because commissioning hadn't been completed before the code took effect, its provisions applied retrospectively, requiring either 'operating plant' status or Type B device certification — neither of which had budgetary provision. "That decision delayed the project by a year and in monetary terms set it back by close to 0.5 million AUD."

International equipment compliance creates another regulatory trap. The Yuri project found that "differences between the design standards and safety regulations of the manufacturing country and those of Australia...required modifications to ensure compliance with Australian Standards. These adjustments led to a significant increase in both time and cost." The Hazer CDP project ordered a fuel cell "before Australian fuel cell standards existed" only to face retrospective compliance with AS 62282-3 when standards were released. "In Q3 2023 these analyses led to the decision that the process to achieve approval...was significant and likely unachievable, and that the existing fuel cell would need to be replaced."

### Technical Underperformance: Manageable When Anticipated

Technical underperformance represents 18% of adverse records but shows only 12% severity escalation — the lowest of any failure mode. This suggests technical issues, while common, are typically manageable when properly anticipated and resourced.

The integration of renewable energy with electrolysers presents the most common technical challenge. The Yuri project found that "the electrolyser OEM has only very limited experience in flexible operating conditions" driven by variable renewable input. This created challenges in "starting the plant each day, ramping up the electrolyser, and ultimately producing hydrogen at specification." At reduced loads during solar variability, "lye pumps operating at constant full-load flow rates risked inefficient gas-liquid separation, potentially allowing H2 and O2 gases to accumulate in the lye loop and creating an explosion risk."

Equipment reliability from international suppliers emerges as a consistent issue. The Denham project found that "despite utilising equipment manufactured by market-leading companies such as Nel, PowerCell and Veolia, the project had equipment failures prior to operational handover." The Hazer CDP experienced multiple material failures in the exotic Incoloy 617 reactor due to "defective source material" from Chinese suppliers, requiring re-order from "Tier 1 European and North American suppliers" and "13 additional months of fabrication."

However, these technical challenges are predictable and manageable with proper risk management. Projects that planned for technical complexity — such as the Yarwun hydrogen calcination pilot that "invested in updating or enhancing Operator Training Simulators before introducing significant process changes" — successfully identified and resolved control logic errors before live commissioning.

## Failure Mode Deep-Dives

### Commercial & Market: The Fundamental Challenge

Commercial failures dominate the hydrogen sector because the technology has reached technical feasibility faster than commercial viability. The evidence shows projects consistently achieving technical milestones while failing to close the commercial gap.

The cost structure is unforgiving. As PGH2 found, "electricity price and HPF capital intensity are the two dominant LCOH drivers, together accounting for approximately 84 percent of total LCOH (43% and 41% respectively)." This creates a double bind: renewable electricity costs must fall while electrolyser costs must fall simultaneously to achieve viable economics.

The Port Augusta Solar Methanol project identified a structural problem with sub-scale economics: "The sub-scale 7,500 tonne per annum project scope resulted in high per-unit capital and operating costs, with processing equipment most expensive on a $/MW basis at small scale and an operational workforce requirement similar in size to a much larger plant." Yet scaling up requires offtake commitments that aren't available at current cost levels.

Market immaturity compounds the problem. As HVHH Phase 2 noted, "markets for low carbon hydrogen have not developed as quickly as expected. In particular for this project demand for hydrogen for the mobility sector has not materialised." The Gibson Island project found that "the economics for the Project remain challenging, with power pricing and the price customers are willing to pay proving to be a significant hurdle to commercial viability."

### Regulatory & Approvals: Complexity and Uncertainty

Regulatory challenges in hydrogen go beyond normal project approval delays. The sector faces fundamental regulatory gaps where existing frameworks don't accommodate hydrogen use cases.

The complexity is staggering. The Gibson Island project faced "37 approvals across 19 agencies for the hydrogen facility alone, reflecting a high degree of complexity stemming from the combined use of hydrogen electrolysis and ammonia synthesis." The project noted that "the absence of a dedicated legislative framework for large-scale green hydrogen projects led to protracted assessment periods."

International equipment compliance creates systematic problems. The New Energies Service Station project found that "overseas electrolyser and refuelling equipment vendors struggled to comply with Australian Standards, particularly AS/NZS 3000:2018 (Wiring Rules), which is not derived from an international standard." The Yuri project required "significant rework of engineering deliverables from Chinese vendors" due to "compliance to Project standards and documentation gaps between Chinese and Australian / Western requirements."

The Hydrogen Park Murray Valley project spent "significantly more time and effort" on electrolyser localisation, requiring "more than 6 trips to several manufacturing facilities" over "approximately 6 months longer than expected" to achieve Australian compliance.

### Execution & Logistics: International Supply Chain Reality

Execution problems represent 16% of adverse records with 23% severity escalation. The core issue is Australia's dependence on international supply chains for hydrogen equipment, combined with vendors' unfamiliarity with Australian requirements.

Lead time optimism is endemic. The Yuri project found that "lead times communicated by the hydrogen equipment suppliers during the tendering phase were overly optimistic and did not appropriately consider contingencies for design verification, adaptation to Australian standards and flexible operation." The New Energies Service Station project experienced "electrolyser vendor lead times [ranging] from 12–18 months for standard packages, with an additional 6 months required for Australian market compliance gap analysis, resulting in overall lead times of 18–24 months."

Quality control across international supply chains is challenging. The Yuri project discovered "on numerous occasions that Project specification requirements were often not cascaded to sub-vendors of international suppliers." The Hazer CDP project learned that "physical on-site inspection by company personnel is essential" after material defects weren't detected despite third-party TUV certification.

### Coordination & Stakeholders: Managing Complexity

Coordination failures account for 10% of adverse records but create cascading effects across other dimensions. The multi-technology, multi-party nature of hydrogen projects creates interface management challenges.

Early contractor involvement is essential but often inadequate. The Gibson Island project found that "vendors of key hydrogen equipment...were also not experienced with onshore assembly and commissioning of their systems in Australia, compounding the lack of local precedent." The CQ-H2 project noted that "market engagement with major global construction contractors revealed very limited appetite for a single EPC contractor to assume design, construction, and performance risk for a first-of-a-kind large-scale renewable hydrogen development."

Interface management between renewable generation and hydrogen production creates specific challenges. The PGH2 project identified "schedule misalignment between the independently developed VRE (wind and solar) and HPF components...with interdependent projects potentially not reaching Commercial Operation Date in respective timeframes due to approval or critical-path item lags."

## What Has Changed Over Time

The hydrogen sector has evolved significantly across the 2012-2024 period, with distinct patterns emerging in different phases.

### Early Period (2012-2021): Technology Optimism

Early ARENA hydrogen R&D projects (funded pre-2021) suffered from what can now be identified as systematic overoptimism. A review of 16 prior projects totalling $22.1 million identified recurring failures where "projects lacked sufficient commercialisation resources and funds, causing promising technologies to stall in the gap between research validation and commercial deployment."

Cost projections from this period proved unrealistic. The ATCO ScaleH2 study observed that "previous green hydrogen cost projections were overly optimistic and have not been realised, with costs now expected to range between AU$8.25–$13.20/kg in central Europe by 2030, significantly above earlier forecasts below AU$4.95/kg."

### Transition Period (2019-2021): Reality Check

Projects entering FEED or construction during 2019-2021 encountered the first wave of reality checks. The Edify project noted that "anticipated downward pricing trends for key electrolyser and hydrogen plant equipment did not eventuate between 2022 and 2025. OEMs had not achieved step-change cost reductions because the anticipated large increase in demand had not materialised."

COVID-19 accelerated this reality check by exposing supply chain vulnerabilities. The QUT H2Xport project noted this was "a Force Majeure situation and contractually no provision had been made for it in any of the equipment supplier/services contracts or indeed even in the ARENA agreement."

### Current Period (2022-2024): Commercial Maturation

Recent projects show a more mature understanding of commercial challenges but also demonstrate that the fundamental economics remain unresolved. The Hunter Valley Hydrogen Hub Phase 2 observed that "inflationary and supply-chain pressures escalated capital costs across the Australian renewable hydrogen sector, compounding the challenge of making large-scale decarbonisation projects economically viable."

Projects now routinely plan for government support as a necessity rather than an option. The PGH2 study concluded that "leveraging government support through grants, subsidies, and tax credits will help narrow the cost gap between green hydrogen and its current methods of production."

## Due Diligence Checklist

### Commercial Viability Deep Dive

**What is your LCOH at current electrolyser prices and renewable electricity costs, and who has agreed to pay it?** The Edify project's LCOH of $26-33/kg found "nearly all potential offtakers unable or unwilling to pay a green premium." If your LCOH exceeds $10/kg, probe the realism of the commercial case. Ask for evidence of offtaker willingness-to-pay at your price point, not just expressions of interest.

**Have you modelled the economics without government support?** CQ-H2's "optimised hydrogen production price of $7.39/kg...did not incorporate any potential grant funding, concessional funding or the Hydrogen Production Tax Incentive." Projects that cannot demonstrate a path to commercial viability without permanent subsidies face stranded asset risk.

**What happens if your electrolyser costs don't decline as forecast?** The Edify project found that "anticipated downward pricing trends for key electrolyser...equipment did not eventuate between 2022 and 2025." Use current demonstrated costs rather than forecast cost reductions as your base case.

### Regulatory Path Validation

**Does a legal approval pathway exist for your specific hydrogen application?** The H2RESTORE project found that underground hydrogen storage "falls outside the [Petroleum] Act's definition of petroleum," creating "project-stopping regulatory uncertainty." Identify all required approvals and confirm legal pathways exist before committing to FEED expenditure.

**Have you validated international equipment compliance requirements at the component level?** The Yuri project required "significant rework of engineering deliverables from Chinese vendors" due to Australian Standards gaps. Require vendors to demonstrate AS3000, ASME, and other Australian compliance before contract award, not after delivery.

**What regulatory changes are in consultation or development that could affect your project?** The QUT project was caught by Queensland's hydrogen safety code that "had been in circulation for stakeholder consultation for over a year before it came into effect." Track emerging regulatory codes from consultation stage, not just notification date.

### Execution Risk Management

**What are your vendor-quoted lead times, and have you added compliance adaptation time?** The New Energies Service Station project experienced "overall lead times of 18–24 months" including "an additional 6 months required for Australian market compliance." Add 50% contingency to vendor-quoted electrolyser lead times for Australian delivery.

**How will you manage international supply chain compliance at the sub-vendor level?** The Yuri project found "on numerous occasions that Project specification requirements were often not cascaded to sub-vendors of international suppliers." Allocate dedicated QAQC personnel based overseas to monitor vendor and sub-vendor chains.

**What is your Force Majeure and supply chain disruption plan?** The QUT project noted "contractually no provision had been made" for COVID disruptions. Include Force Majeure provisions in all supplier contracts and maintain multiple supplier relationships for critical components.

### Integration and Technology Risk

**Have you validated electrolyser operation under your specific renewable energy profile?** The Denham project found that electrolyser "operational constraints...were incompatible with the project's solar-dispatch use case, requiring development of novel dispatch control algorithms." Test electrolyser dispatch algorithms under your actual renewable generation profile, not standard profiles.

**What is your plan for managing system integration between hydrogen production, renewable generation, and offtake?** The East Kimberley project identified that "the hydrogen pipeline sizing, operational flexibility of the Ammonia Production Facility, and the firm energy mix" required further development. These interdependencies should be resolved during FEED, not deferred to detailed design.

**How will you manage equipment reliability for overseas-manufactured equipment with limited local support?** The Denham project experienced "multiple failures across all three major equipment types requiring repair or full replacement before operational handover." Specify extended warranties, factory acceptance testing, and local spare parts inventory for critical overseas equipment.

This checklist should be applied iteratively at each project gate. The evidence shows that hydrogen project risks compound rather than resolve over time, making early identification and mitigation essential for success.
