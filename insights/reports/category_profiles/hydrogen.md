# Hydrogen — Delivery Risk Profile

## Executive Summary

Hydrogen projects face three dominant delivery risks: commercial viability gaps that have caused multiple project cancellations, complex regulatory frameworks lacking mature approval pathways, and technical integration challenges from immature equipment supply chains. Commercial risks are the most severe—25% of adverse records relate to market and financing challenges, with 45% escalating to major or critical severity. The regulatory landscape remains fragmented across jurisdictions, while international equipment procurement introduces compliance delays averaging 6+ months beyond planned schedules.

## Coverage and Data Quality

This analysis draws from 1,418 records across 81 hydrogen projects, with strong representation from 2022–2024 (824 records, 53 projects). Recent records dominate the evidence base, providing current insight into market conditions, regulatory frameworks, and technology maturity. 44 records (3.1%) carry temporal warnings indicating pre-2021 content about fast-moving technology areas. The dataset spans the full project lifecycle from R&D through operations, with particularly strong coverage of design, procurement, and regulatory approval phases.

## Risk Landscape by Delivery Dimension

**Financing emerges as the highest-risk dimension**, with 81% of financing-related adverse records attributed to commercial and market failure modes. The Hunter Valley Hydrogen Hub Phase 2 and Central Queensland Hydrogen Project (CQ-H2) exemplify this pattern—both advanced projects were discontinued in 2025 despite significant FEED investment, unable to bridge the commercial viability gap. Large-scale renewable hydrogen projects consistently struggle with levelised hydrogen costs of $7–33/kg against limited industrial willingness to pay green premiums.

**Design represents the largest absolute volume of adverse records** (510 records), driven by technical underperformance (29%) and regulatory compliance gaps (19%). International equipment procurement dominates these challenges—Chinese electrolyser packages consistently require 6+ months of additional compliance work for Australian standards, while vendor lead times prove systematically optimistic without accounting for design verification and adaptation requirements.

**Procurement risks center on supply chain immaturity and compliance gaps.** Execution and logistics failures account for 31% of procurement adverse records, driven by international vendor coordination challenges. The Yuri project exemplifies this pattern: overseas vendors repeatedly failed to cascade project requirements to sub-vendors, particularly for flexible/intermittent operation requirements, causing significant delays and rework.

**Regulatory and approvals challenges are pervasive but variable by jurisdiction.** Grid connection faces the highest regulatory impact (66% of grid connection records), while siting and design phases also show material regulatory exposure. Victoria's fragmented hydrogen approval framework—where underground hydrogen storage lacks legislative pathway under the Petroleum Act 1998—demonstrates how regulatory gaps can become project-stopping risks.

**Integration and commissioning carries moderate but consistent risk**, with 21% of records attributed to coordination failures and 24% to execution challenges. Projects consistently underestimate the complexity of integrating renewable generation, electrolysis, compression, and end-use systems, particularly under variable operating conditions.

## Failure Mode Deep-Dive

### Commercial and Market Failures (25% of adverse records, 45% severe)

Commercial risks have proven project-stopping across multiple large-scale developments. CQ-H2's discontinuation after completing FEED demonstrates how first-of-kind projects face fundamental commercial barriers that engineering solutions alone cannot resolve. The project achieved Class 4 design definition but could not secure offtake at a viable price point, with hydrogen production costs around $7.39/kg requiring substantial government support mechanisms to achieve commercial viability.

The Edify Green Hydrogen Project illustrates the "chicken and egg" problem endemic to hydrogen market development: offtakers require project maturity and price certainty before committing, while projects need offtake certainty for FID. Despite extensive engagement across maritime, rail, mobility, and industrial sectors, no offtake agreement was secured during FEED, with the levelised cost of hydrogen at $26–33/kg far exceeding conventional fuel costs.

ScaleH2's commercial assessment revealed that Australian projects face structural disadvantages in European auction mechanisms, which favour existing ammonia plants or near-complete facilities over greenfield developments. The study found that successful H2Global auction projects typically range from 66–300 ktpa production, while ScaleH2's 800 ktpa capacity exceeded single HPA contract sizes, risking inability to contract full production capacity.

### Regulatory and Approvals Failures (22% of adverse records, 29% severe)

Regulatory complexity stems from hydrogen's intersection with multiple framework gaps rather than prescriptive barriers. Victoria's underground hydrogen storage exemplifies this challenge—the H2RESTORE project identified that the Petroleum Act 1998 does not permit UHS because hydrogen falls outside the definition of petroleum, requiring legislative amendment before project progression.

Queensland's introduction of the Hydrogen Safety Code of Practice in August 2023 caught the QUT H2Xport project unprepared, requiring either 'operating plant' status or Type B gas device certification. The retrospective application of these provisions added approximately $0.5 million in costs and a one-year delay, demonstrating how regulatory change pipelines create material schedule risk.

Grid connection regulatory challenges reflect infrastructure framework mismatches with large new loads. CQ-H2's grid connection required new transmission infrastructure to Larcom Creek substation under a regulatory regime better suited to incremental expansions than large, complex first-mover projects requiring substantial augmentation.

### Technical Underperformance (18% of adverse records, 12% severe)

Technical risks concentrate in electrolyser integration with variable renewable generation and Australian standards compliance. The Yuri project revealed that alkaline electrolyser OEMs have very limited experience with flexible operating conditions, causing significant engineering effort in developing control system logic, operating procedures, and daily start/stop sequencing.

International equipment adaptation consistently exceeds expectations. Hydrogen Park Murray Valley's Chinese alkaline electrolyser required approximately 6 months longer than the planned 10-month compliance timeline, requiring more than 6 trips to manufacturing facilities and extensive sub-component supplier engagement. The extent of design reviews, third-party verifications, and documentation translation cycles proved very difficult to estimate.

The Yuri project identified that at reduced electrolyser loads, lye pumps operating at constant flow rates risk inefficient gas-liquid separation, potentially allowing H₂ and O₂ gases to accumulate in the lye loop and creating explosion risk. Variable speed drives for lye circulation pumps became a non-negotiable design requirement not identified by the OEM without owner-driven analysis.

### Execution and Logistics Failures (16% of adverse records, 23% severe)

Execution risks are dominated by international supply chain coordination and schedule optimism. CQ-H2's CAPEX estimate grew 45% from the original HyGATE application to final FEED, rising to $220–$290 million due to inflation, foreign exchange exposure, scope growth, and failure of anticipated electrolyser cost reductions to materialise.

The Yuri project's schedule extended from 19 to 33 months due to compounding factors: engineering rework, vendor delays, construction underestimation, and EPA approval overruns. International OEM vendors repeatedly failed to comply with Australian standards (ASME, AS3000, EEHA), requiring mid-project supplier substitution for the hydrogen compressor.

Vendor supply capacity limitations emerged as a genuine critical-path constraint. Multiple projects experienced delays from electrolyser vendor capacity limitations, reflecting broader market constraints in the rapidly evolving supply market during project procurement phases.

## Temporal Trends

**Commercial risks have intensified rather than diminished**, with multiple large-scale project cancellations in 2024–2025 despite years of industry development. The discontinuation of CQ-H2, Hunter Valley Hydrogen Hub Phase 2, and others signals that commercial viability gaps remain the primary limiting factor. Early ARENA projects experienced cost and market optimism that has since been corrected by demonstrated project economics.

**Regulatory frameworks are gradually maturing but remain fragmented**. Queensland's Hydrogen Safety Code introduction in 2023 exemplifies this evolution—frameworks are being developed but often retrospectively affect projects already in development. Victoria's interim hydrogen planning guidelines (published November 2023) represent progress but have not been formally adopted into planning schemes.

**Technical performance with international equipment has become more predictable but not necessarily faster**. Projects consistently plan 6+ months for equipment adaptation to Australian standards, suggesting learning has occurred but compliance timelines have not compressed. However, the specific nature of technical challenges (lye pump VSDs, thermal cycling analysis, harmonic filters) has become more predictable.

**Offshore market development has proceeded more slowly than anticipated**, with European auction mechanisms favouring existing infrastructure over Australian greenfield developments. The expectation of rapid offtake market development has not materialised, forcing projects to rely increasingly on government support mechanisms.

## Key Watchpoints for Due Diligence

1. **Offtake Security and Pricing**: Validate that offtake agreements exist with creditworthy counterparties at prices that cover production costs without assuming green premiums will materialise. Multiple large projects have been cancelled for lack of viable offtake despite technical readiness.

2. **Regulatory Pathway Completeness**: Confirm that all required approvals have identified pathways under current legislation, particularly for underground storage, high-pressure pipelines, and novel land uses. Projects should not assume framework gaps will be resolved on favourable timelines.

3. **International Equipment Compliance Buffer**: For electrolyser and major equipment packages from international OEMs, verify that 6+ months of additional schedule and budget has been allocated for Australian standards compliance, design verification, and sub-vendor management.

4. **Grid Connection Critical Path**: Assess whether transmission upgrades are required and on what timeline—multiple projects identified 7-year transmission upgrade timelines that exceeded overall project development schedules. Grid connection studies should be initiated early and in parallel with other development activities.

5. **Commercial Scale Economics**: Verify that the project scale unlocks economies of scale or creates additional commercial barriers. Sub-scale projects face disproportionately high per-unit costs, while very large projects exceed single offtaker appetite and struggle with financing.

6. **Government Support Dependency**: Model project economics with and without available government support mechanisms (production tax incentives, grants, concessional finance) and assess the probability that these will be accessible at FID timing.

7. **Force Majeure and Change Management**: Ensure contracts with equipment suppliers, EPC contractors, and funding parties include robust force majeure provisions and change management procedures—COVID-19 and regulatory changes have repeatedly disrupted hydrogen projects without adequate contractual protection.
